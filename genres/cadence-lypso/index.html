Cadence-lypso: Dominican roots, artists and listening guide

Cadence-lypso connects Dominican musicians with the wider Creole-speaking Caribbean and its diaspora. Start with the musicians’ accounts of the sound, then follow the artists, recordings and editions that carry its history.

What does cadence-lypso mean?

In his Mondo Local interview, Exile One founder Gordon Henderson explains the name as a combination of Haitian musical elements and the calypso phrasing of his vocals. He recalls adapting the group’s repertoire for dancers at a Paris performance, drawing on Haitian music the band already knew from playing in the French Antilles.

Henderson describes that performance as a turning point in the group’s Creole identity. His recollection connects musicians from Dominica, a working base in Guadeloupe and an audience in Paris. Following those movements is more useful than assuming that the place associated with a genre, the location of a band and the setting of a particular performance must be identical.

Follow the bands and the solo voices

Writing in Dominica News Online in 2016, Henderson described the genre’s international reach as a collective achievement involving bands and other participants. His account names Exile One, Jeff Joseph and Grammacks, Ophelia, Anthony Gussie and Michele Henderson. He also identifies the Saultone label’s work with Ophelia as an important step beyond an exclusively band-centred presentation.

The same commentary discusses Fraiche as a point of change in Exile One’s recorded music and stage presentation, with both updated sounds and Dominican traditional influences. This is Henderson’s assessment of the album’s place in the genre. It provides a reason to compare different periods of an artist’s catalog instead of treating every release as an example of one fixed sound.

Cadence, bouyon and zouk in the same cultural landscape

The Caribbean Development Bank’s Dominica cultural profile lists cadence-lypso separately from cadence (kadans)/compas. Bouyon and zouk also appear as separate entries in its cultural-forms inventory. That list recognizes several names within Dominica’s musical landscape; it is not a rule for classifying every individual recording.

When browsing, keep the artist, song title and version together. An artist can appear in more than one genre guide. A shared performer or similar title is a useful lead for further listening, but it does not establish that two releases contain the same recording. How Cadence Month brought the history into discussion

At the July 2021 launch of Dominica’s inaugural Cadence Month, organizer Leroy Charles called for greater local recognition and investment. Cultural researcher Gregory Rabess described an educational purpose: helping younger listeners understand the music’s history, influence and continuing evolution. Dominica News Online reported panel discussions and a daily radio history feature among the announced activities.

That educational approach is useful for a listening journey today. Read an artist’s own account beside the tracklist, compare a studio edition with a clearly labelled live performance, and keep historical release dates separate from later reissue dates. Artist profiles provide starting points; full catalog pages show the named sources and the gaps that remain.
