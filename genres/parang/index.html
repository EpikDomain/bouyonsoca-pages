Parang music: Trinidad’s Christmas traditions, instruments and soca connections

Parang is closely associated with Christmas in Trinidad and Tobago. Follow the house-visiting tradition, the purpose of different song forms and specific recordings to explore its place in Caribbean music.

A Christmas tradition with more than one origin account

NALIS, Trinidad and Tobago’s national library authority, connects parang with Spanish parranda and Venezuelan Christmas visiting traditions. It presents two origin accounts: transmission through Spanish colonial settlement and later migration by Venezuelan cocoa workers. Its guide does not settle on one exclusive founding story.

Parranderos visit households with music, dancing and shared hospitality. NALIS describes a season stretching from October to January 6. That cultural calendar is not a verified schedule of this year’s concerts.

Sources: NALIS — Parang

Arrival, storytelling and farewell

The NALIS guide distinguishes an arrival song, or serenal, from a despedida that thanks the host and marks departure. It also describes song categories about the Annunciation and Nativity, alongside secular forms. This helps explain why a parang performance can serve several purposes within one gathering.

Its instrument overview includes cuatro, guitar, box bass and maracas among traditional ensemble combinations. The guide also discusses a separate French-Creole Christmas-song tradition called Cantiques de Noël. Do not assume that every seasonal recording has the same language or instrumentation.

Sources: NALIS — Parang

Daisy Voisin and La Divina Pastora

NALIS remembers Daisy Voisin as a central parang performer whose work with La Divina Pastora travelled beyond Trinidad and Tobago. It names Sereno Sereno and El Nacimiento de la Verdad among her compositions and identifies Alegria Alegria as her favorite, with musician Leroy Birch helping complete it.

Those names provide a starting point for exploring Voisin’s repertoire. A composition credit and a recording credit answer different questions: a later performance of one of these songs may involve another singer or ensemble. Check the specific release before assigning its performers.

Sources: NALIS — Daisy Voisin biography

An instrumental example from the archive

Smithsonian Folkways lists Parang by the Grand Curucaye String Orchestra of Trinidad on Epilogue to the String Band Tradition. Its catalog gives a 1956 release year, a duration of 2:29 and the identifier COOK05020_202. It labels the track instrumental.

The credited instruments include piano, cuatro, acoustic guitar, violin, bandol, box bass, guero and flute, but the players are listed as unspecified. Keep that gap visible: an instrument list is not evidence for naming individual musicians. The label’s track page offers an exact reference for this performance rather than a general playlist.

Sources: Smithsonian Folkways — Parang, Grand Curucaye String Orchestra of Trinidad

Where soca parang enters the picture

TTT’s December 2025 introduction to an interview with Edwin “Crazy” Ayoung describes soca parang as a fusion of soca and parang. The broadcaster’s page links the interview for listeners who want to hear Ayoung discuss it.

Use that fusion label with the particular artist or recording in view. Traditional parang, an instrumental archive track and a soca parang performance need not share every feature. Explore the soca guide alongside this page, then follow the credited version of a song rather than treating every Christmas mix as one genre.
